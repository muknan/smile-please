# Tagline / brand-copy research memo — September 2026

**Assignment.** Select one short human tagline for a Delhi dental-health nonprofit. This is messaging research, not legal trademark clearance. Web checks were run on 17 September 2026; search indexing is incomplete, so absence of a result is not proof of availability.

## Decision criteria supported by authoritative guidance

1. **Plain and immediately understandable.** CDC says health information should be understandable and usable by its audience; its plain-language checklist recommends putting the most important message first, using familiar words, active voice, short sentences, and one idea at a time. [CDC, Plain Language Materials & Resources](https://www.cdc.gov/health-literacy/php/develop-materials/plain-language.html)
2. **Positive, respectful, and inclusive.** CDC’s equity-centered communication principles call for clear/plain language, culturally responsive content, positive actions and community strengths, and non-stigmatizing communication. [CDC, Guiding Principles for Equity-Centered Public Health Communication](https://www.cdc.gov/pcd/issues/2023/23_0061.htm)
3. **Accessible and actionable, not merely attractive.** CDC’s health-literacy guidance says materials should be accurate, accessible, and actionable, and recommends developing/testing messages with intended audiences. [CDC, Develop & Test Materials](https://www.cdc.gov/health-literacy/php/develop-materials/develop-test-materials.html)
4. **Use everyday words and feedback.** NIH’s health-literacy handout recommends common everyday language, active voice, jargon-free wording, and testing messages with the intended audience. [NIH, 4 tips to improve your health literacy skills](https://www.nih.gov/sites/default/files/institutes/clear-communication/health-literacy-tips-updated.pdf)
5. **Keep the line human and specific enough to signal the benefit.** CDC communication guidance emphasizes clarifying the goal and audience, keeping the message short, speaking directly, and answering “what’s in it for me?” [CDC/NIOSH, Tips for Communicating Effectively](https://cdc.gov/niosh/healthcare/communication-resources/communicating-effectively.html)

## Collision check: candidate concept

### Exact phrase

Searches for **“Bringing healthier smiles within reach”** and **“healthier smiles within reach”** returned no prominent exact-match organization tagline in the reviewed indexed results. This is only an indexed-web observation, not clearance.

### Near-exact / material conceptual collisions

- **Wake Smiles** (community dental nonprofit/service) uses: “bring healthy smiles within reach for our Wake County community.” [Wake Smiles fundraising page, Aug–Sep 2026](https://www.zeffy.com/en-US/ticketing/dr-siegels-art-raffle)
- **America’s ToothFairy** (national nonprofit) uses “put healthy smiles within reach for children who need it most.” [America’s ToothFairy, Smile Drive 2025 impact](https://www.americastoothfairy.org/stories/smile-drive-2025-impact)
- **Health Canada** tested “Bringing smiles within reach” for the Canadian Dental Care Plan. Its focus-testing report says “Accessible. Affordable. Essential.” was the most motivating option, while “Bringing smiles within reach” was one of the tested statements. [Health Canada, Canadian Dental Care Plan Focus Testing Report](https://publications.gc.ca/collections/collection_2024/sc-hc/H14-580-2024-eng.pdf)
- **Park Dental** uses “Bringing healthier smiles to Inver Grove Heights since 1984.” [Park Dental location page](https://www.parkdental.com/location/park-dental-salem-square/)
- **Brush Buddies Foundation** describes its work as “bringing healthier smiles and renewed confidence” to underserved communities. [Brush Buddies, What We Do](https://www.brushbuddies.org/our-goal)

**Synthesis.** The proposed sentence is understandable and warm, but its two most distinctive semantic pieces—“healthier smiles” and “within reach”—are already common in dental-access and nonprofit language. The risk is not that the exact four-word construction is visibly owned by one prominent organization; it is that the line may feel generic and be difficult to own in search, fundraising, or partner contexts.

## Candidate implications

- **“Bringing healthier smiles within reach.”** Strong on warmth and access; weakest on distinctiveness because it combines two heavily reused dental/nonprofit motifs. Keep only if the organization wants a broad, institution-like promise and local audience testing finds it memorable.
- **“Healthier smiles, closer to home.”** Preserves access and human warmth while moving away from the exact “within reach” collision. It should be checked against Delhi’s actual delivery model; “closer to home” implies local/community-based care.
- **“Care for every Delhi smile.”** More locally ownable and inclusive, with clear place signal. It is less explicit about affordability/access and should be paired with a supporting descriptor if those are central to the mission.
- **“Dental care within reach.”** Clearer and more literal than “smiles,” but generic and somewhat service-like. It may work better as a program descriptor than as the emotional lead line.

## Practical recommendation

Use CDC/NIH criteria as the acceptance test: one short line, everyday words, positive and non-stigmatizing, understandable on first read, and tested with Delhi community members in the languages/channels the nonprofit actually uses. Treat **“Bringing healthier smiles within reach”** as a viable draft but not a differentiated final: the near-exact collision pattern is substantial. Of the alternatives above, **“Healthier smiles, closer to home.”** best preserves the intended human/access promise while reducing direct phrase overlap; validate the geographic promise before adoption.

## Final decision addendum

The implemented line is **“Healthier smiles for more people.”** A follow-up exact-phrase search found no prominent indexed dental or health organization using that complete line. Conceptual overlap remains, as expected in this category: Delta Dental of Colorado Foundation has described its work as helping “more people have healthier smiles,” but not as a brand tagline. [Delta Dental of Colorado Foundation, Funding Available for Oral Health Equity](https://blog.deltadentalco.com/2018/07/funding-available-oral-health/)

This line won because it carries the founder’s inclusive “each and every face” idea forward without claiming universal capacity, uses plain everyday words, clearly signals oral health, and avoids the crowded “within reach” and “closer to home” constructions.

## Uncertainty and limits

- Search results reflect indexed public pages available on 17 September 2026, not trademark databases, domain/social handles, app stores, or offline use.
- The cited organizations vary in scale and legal status; their phrase use demonstrates market-language overlap, not infringement or ownership.
- No Delhi-specific audience testing was available in this research pass. That testing should decide between emotional “smiles” language and more literal “dental care” language.
